Hello, I am

Nathan Steinle

and I'm a

working at the intersection of machine learning, Bayesian statistics, and astrophysics. My recent research builds new models of compact binary systems for multi-messenger science, and develops and validates inference methods for problems where the data are noisy, the parameters are degenerate, and the uncertainty estimate has to be trustworthy rather than merely reported — recently, classifier comparison on learned latent representations, simulation-calibrated Bayesian inference, and amortized posterior estimation with normalizing flows. I hold a PhD in Physics from UT Dallas and completed postdoctoral fellowships at the University of Birmingham and the University of Manitoba, and I am a member of the LIGO–Virgo–KAGRA Collaboration and the LISA Consortium. Alongside research, I have more than ten years of teaching experience spanning university courses, laboratory instruction, and high school physics, and I have supervised over ten students from high school through PhD. I am based in Houston, Texas. Please see my Google Scholar page for publications, or get in touch about collaborations, positions, or questions about astrophysics.

Projects

  1. Classification under severe class overlap: A controlled comparison of supervised, semi-supervised, and unsupervised classifiers on a learned latent representation, across datasets of increasing class overlap and scored on four metrics. Physical Review D 112, 103038 (2025) — PRD | arXiv
  2. Simulation-calibrated Bayesian inference: An iterative calibration framework around a nested sampler for a ten-dimensional inverse problem, raising the fraction of valid posterior samples from 0.36 to 0.83 and documenting the failure of a standard MCMC baseline on the same likelihood. Physical Review D 113, 023020 (2026) — PRD | arXiv
  3. Amortized posterior estimation with normalizing flows: Conditional normalizing flows with a custom convolutional embedding network, validated with simulation-based calibration, PP plots, and empirical coverage at four credible levels, and tested on an independent simulated population. Under review at Physical Review D — arXiv:2607.12971
  4. Graph theory in black hole astrophysics: Ongoing collaboration with the University of Manitoba mathematics department on graph-theoretic methods for learning and regression on noisy, high-dimensional astrophysical datasets.
  5. Computer vision: Image classification of supernova remnant datasets, which are sparsely populated and require advanced statistical techniques, with three PhD students and collaborators at the University of Manitoba.
