About Me

Astrophysicist!

About Me Physicist, machine learning researcher, educator My research is about inference: extracting physical parameters from measurements that are noisy, incomplete, and degenerate, and establishing how much confidence those extractions deserve. The application domain is astrophysics — compact binaries, black holes, gravitational-wave sources — but the methods are general, and the part of the work I care most about is evaluation. That emphasis shows up concretely in what I publish. In a classifier comparison study, k-means returned an F1 score around 0.5 while the adjusted Rand index and normalized mutual information sat near zero; read together, that is not a mediocre classifier but a near-random assignment that F1 alone flatters. I confirmed it three ways — an ablation on raw versus learned features, substituting a Gaussian mixture to rule out an algorithm-specific artifact, and five random initializations to expose seed sensitivity — and published it as a negative result. In a separate study, I ran an emcee MCMC baseline against nested sampling on an identical likelihood; the MCMC output looked smoother and more plausible on inspection but returned essentially no valid samples under every setting tried. I published that too, with an explicit warning that bare MCMC samplers can converge confidently far from the target in complex degenerate parameter spaces. My most recent work uses conditional normalizing flows for amortized posterior estimation, validated with a full calibration suite: simulation-based calibration via posterior rank statistics, PP plots against the uniform diagonal, empirical coverage at the 50%, 68%, 90% and 95% credible levels, and an out-of-distribution test on a population generated by a different pipeline than the training data. A 90% credible interval has to contain the truth 90% of the time, or the uncertainty attached to a result is decorative. Technical practice Python is my primary language, with PyTorch, scikit-learn, SciPy and pandas; I also work in Matlab, Mathematica, C++ and Fortran. Computation runs on HPC clusters via Slurm and HTCondor, with MPI parallelization. I version and containerize with Git and Docker/Singularity, and work primarily in Linux. Collaboration, review and mentoring I am a member of the LIGO–Virgo–KAGRA Collaboration and the LISA Consortium, where I coordinate a project developing data catalogs of accreting ultra-compact binaries. I have refereed for five physics and astronomy journals over four years — rubric-based evaluation of methodology against stated criteria, returned as structured written critique, which is a skill I value as highly as producing results. I have supervised and mentored more than ten students from high school through PhD. One undergraduate I advised led a paper in Physical Review D; several projects with PhD student collaborators have reached publication; and I currently work with colleagues in the University of Manitoba mathematics department on graph-theoretic applications to black hole astrophysics. Teaching I have taught across the full range of levels: instructor of record for university classical mechanics, co-lecturer in multi-messenger astronomy, guest lecturer in gravitational-wave and radio astronomy, several years of electromagnetism laboratory instruction, and most recently AP Physics 1 at St. John’s School in Houston during the fall 2026 semester. Teaching at the college level, where instruction and active research reinforce each other, is where I do my best work. Currently I am based in Houston, Texas, and open to research, applied machine learning and data science, and college-level teaching positions in the Houston area or remote.
